# TODO: candidate analyses

The research-backed backlog of checks considered but not yet
implemented. Implemented checks are documented in
[analyses.md](analyses.md); patterns that will never be implemented
(with the soundness argument against each) are in its
[rejected-folds appendix](analyses.md#appendix-folds-rejected-for-soundness).
Sources: the Arm Software Optimization Guides (Neoverse N1/V2), the
Apple Silicon CPU Optimization Guide, and gaps noted while building
the existing checks.

Population figures marked **2026-08 sweep** come from
`tools/shapescan.py` over six macOS Mach-O binaries totalling 28.4M
instructions: OpenSSL 3.6.3 `libcrypto` (561k), `/usr/lib/dyld`
arm64e (162k), `go` 1.26.7 (1.47M), `librustc_driver` stable (26.0M),
`/bin/bash` arm64e (118k) and `/usr/bin/ssh` arm64e (113k).
librustc_driver is 91% of that total, so read the per-binary numbers,
not just the sum. Every encoding mask the sweep uses is verified
against the assembler by `shapescan.py --selftest`, and each non-zero
population below was spot-checked against real disassembly -- both
steps caught mask and modelling errors that had inflated earlier
counts by one to three orders of magnitude.

`--selftest` verifies masks in both directions. The collision half
catches a mask that is too loose, which fails loudly by reporting an
absurd number. The recall half catches a mask that is too narrow,
which fails silently: it reports a plausible small number, and nothing
looks wrong. Every population below is therefore an undercount until
some reference instruction proves each spelling of its shape is
matched -- both register widths, scaled and unscaled addressing,
integer and SIMD&FP register classes. The recall half was added after
the LDUR/STUR blindness in the LDP/STP coalescer (a77f2fa) showed the
same class of error could sit in the scanners; it immediately found
six such masks here, one of which had been undercounting by 3.9x.

Neither half catches a mask that leaks into **unallocated** encoding
space. The collision half asks whether a mask matches some *other
reference instruction*, and an unallocated word is in nobody's
reference set, so the mask passes and the scanner reports data as
code. That is what put six phantom sites into the dead-write row
below. Two habits follow: pin the bits that separate a class from the
unallocated slots inside it, not just from its neighbours, and prefer
a `MASKS` registry entry to an inline mask -- `--selftest` only ever
sees the registry, so anything built inline (as `_pure_writer`'s set
was) is unverified by construction.

Where a candidate needed operand conditions finer than one shape mask
can carry, it has its own scanner held to the same discipline:
`tools/addpairscan.py` splits the ADD + LDP/STP family into the half
that folds 2-for-1 and the half whose offset overflows the pair's
imm7, applies each half's own encodability condition, reports which
register the split would free, and carries its own `--selftest`.

## Coverage gaps in shipped checks

The `--selftest` discipline above proves a *scanner's* masks cover
every spelling of their shape. It says nothing about which spellings
and register classes a *check's* decoder chain accepts, and that is a
separate hole with the same failure mode: a check reports a plausible
number off a fraction of its own population, and nothing looks wrong.
Two of the three rows below were found only by measuring a shipped
check against the shape it claims to cover, the same way a77f2fa's
LDUR blindness was.

The first two were measured by lowering `check_add_ldr_str_multi_fold`'s
minimum-uses threshold from 2 to 1 and re-running the corpus, which
reuses that check's real deadness proof and side-entry gate instead of
approximating them. That yields **10,493** single-use sites against the
**8,428** `check_add_ldr_imm_offset` reports, and the residue splits
cleanly by what the older check cannot see:

```
int      adjacent   5529      covered
fp-pair  adjacent   2340      covered (its pair arm reads both files)
int-pair adjacent    226      covered
fp       adjacent   1782  <-  single SIMD&FP access: integer-only
gapped   (all)       616  <-  strict adjacency refuses these
```

Both arrows are now shipped, and the two predictions behaved
differently in a way worth keeping. The gapped row came in at exactly
616 because it was measured through the same check that now reports
it -- same window, same deadness proof, same gate -- so the model and
the implementation were the same code. The SIMD&FP row came in at
1,830 against 1,782 because it was measured through that check but
implemented in the other one, whose strict adjacency needs no
un-clobbered-source condition where a window does. A prediction is
exact only when it is made by the machinery that will realize it.

The third row was measured a third way -- by counting the encoding
shape in the corpus, with no deadness proof applied -- and its 225 was
never a prediction of findings, only of candidates. It shipped as 18,
which is right: its already-covered twin realizes 199 of 3,235, and
the two rates agree to within two points. All three rows are now
closed. What the class had in common is worth keeping: every one was a
check reporting a plausible number off a fraction of its own
population, with nothing in the output to say so, and every one was
found by measuring a shipped check against the shape it claims rather
than by reading it.

| --- | --- |
| Restrict the scan to the symbol table's function ranges | The scan walks whole executable sections, so anything in them that *decodes* -- literal pools, jump tables, alignment islands -- is linted as code. This file already records what that costs: six of the ten surviving "pure write immediately clobbered" sites were four copies of SHA-1's round constant `0x5A827999` in libcrypto's and dyld's literal pools, and the LDR-literal row had to resolve and decode every target before it could say the premise was wrong. x86lint restricts to `.symtab` function ranges by default, tallies the excluded bytes into its skipped count, prints the restriction in the summary, and takes `-a` to scan everything anyway; it refuses `.dynsym` for this (exports only, so restricting to them would silently miss almost all code) and so stripped binaries scan whole sections exactly as now. armlint already parses both Mach-O `nlist`/`LC_FUNCTION_STARTS` and the ELF symbol tables for symbolization -- the boundaries are in hand, the scan simply does not gate on them. Caveat measured 2026-09: Debian's ELF arm64 binaries carry no `.symtab` at all, so the restriction is a no-op there, exactly as it is for a stripped x86 binary |
| BTI landing-pad verification (`-b`, the `-e` analog) | The largest genuinely new capability, and the one whose port is nearly mechanical. `GNU_PROPERTY_AARCH64_FEATURE_1_AND`'s BTI bit is the exact counterpart of x86's IBT bit, so a binary makes the same two claims that can drift apart: the note says every indirect target is padded, and the code either honours that or does not. All seven evidence classes carry over unchanged -- the entry point of a `PT_INTERP` program, `DT_INIT`/`DT_FINI`, the `{pre,}init_array` slots, `JUMP_SLOT`/`GLOB_DAT` relocations resolving in-object, `IRELATIVE` resolvers, every defined dynamic function symbol, and baked absolute pointers landing in an executable section (counted only when they address a known function entry, since a compare-only label legitimately has no pad) -- as do the four verdicts, including the one worth the most: pads present but the note lost, which the linker's AND produces from a single assembly object built without the flag. armlint's census already tallies BTI hints but never reconciles them against the note. AArch64 admits a sharper check than x86 has: `bti c`, `bti j` and `bti jc` say which transfer may land, so a `br` target wants `j` or `jc` and a `blr` target `c` or `jc`, and a pad of the wrong flavour is a finding x86 has no way to express |
| `--json` output and an `on_finding` callback | armlint has neither. The callback is the library half (name, absolute address, decoded form, raw bytes, one call per finding, independent of the summary and of `-v`) and `--json` is the driver half built on it. What it unlocks is joining findings against anything else keyed by address, and the join worth making is an execution profile: how often a compiler *emits* a suboptimal encoding is a poor proxy for what it costs, and one inside a JIT's dispatch loop outweighs thousands in cold init code. x86lint's `vaddr` is a number rather than a hex string for exactly that reason, and it streams findings as they are found rather than accumulating them |
| `-f NAME` single-function scan | "Lint just this function", drawing on `.dynsym` as well so an exported function of a stripped library can be named, and scanning every sized symbol spelled NAME (a versioned export has several). Composes with `-i` into the per-variant census: x86lint's worked example runs `-i -f` over each of glibc's three `memmove` IFUNC targets and reads off a different psABI level for each, which is the per-target question a whole-binary census cannot answer |
| By-function summary table | The top ten offending functions with the tail summed into a residue line, so a whole-binary count reads as "and here is where to start". armlint symbolizes individual `-v` findings already, so this is a tally over machinery that exists. Findings in code reached through an unsized assembly label want an honest `outside every function range` row rather than the nearest neighbour |
| Exit code 2 for tool failure | armlint returns 1 both when it finds opportunities and when the invocation or the file is bad, so CI cannot tell a dirty scan from a broken run. x86lint follows the grep convention: 0 clean, 1 findings, 2 tool failure |
| Skipped-word counter in the lint summary | The census reports undecodable words; the lint scan does not, so partial coverage of a data-laden region is indistinguishable from a clean sweep. x86lint exposes it as `x86lint_summary_skipped` on the library path as well |
| Census evidence labelling | The census asks the reader to treat small exotic tallies with suspicion and check `-v` samples by hand. x86lint automates exactly that: every tally is labelled against the bytes the toolchain recorded as code -- sized function symbols, `.eh_frame` FDE pc-ranges, Go `pclntab` boundaries -- a family with hits outside all of it prints as `FAM (n, u unevidenced)`, and a `code evidence` line reports what backed the labels. The semantics are deliberately asymmetric, inside evidence lending trust while unevidenced means only "no toolchain claim". `tools/cohere` is the record of why the cheap alternatives do not work: run length, gap adjacency and self-synchronization consensus all fail on ground truth, a GHC binary's phantom x87 reaching 92% consensus because self-sync is a decoder property and junk converges onto its own stable chain |
| Go `pclntab` parsing | A Go binary carries neither `nlist` nor `LC_FUNCTION_STARTS` nor a useful symbol table, so findings print unannotated and the census's pac-ret coverage line is omitted. The runtime's own function table survives `strip` because traceback needs it, and x86lint parses it across all four header eras back to Go 1.2, finding it by section name or by scanning the data sections and believing it only after the whole functab validates against the executable sections and the entry point (modern linkers store a dead `textStart` word, so the base is recovered from the section layout, and an externally linked binary whose candidates are all wrong drops the table honestly). Porting it gives armlint function boundaries, symbolization and pac-ret coverage on Go binaries at once |

| ~~Multi-use base: fold every access off one ADD, then delete it~~ | **Done, 2026-08: `check_add_ldr_str_multi_fold`, 3,804 findings across 28.4M instructions** (3,188 at two or more uses, and 616 more once the sole gapped use was let in -- see the coverage-gaps row above). Teaching `check_add_ldr_imm_offset` the unscaled spelling made the whole candidate population visible to it but turned only **126** of the 9,124 encodable ADD + LDUR/STUR sites into findings. The gap was never blindness, it was the deadness tier: the immediate tier is a load into its own base, 21.3% of the encodable *scaled* population and 1.4% of the unscaled one. The unscaled spelling appears where the offset is negative or off the access-size grid -- a field access off a base the ADD does not consume, and at 10.2% of unscaled sites the very next instruction is another access off the same base against 4.4% of scaled ones. So the fold those wanted was N-for-1: when *every* use of the ADD's Rd is an access whose combined offset encodes, all of them rebase and the ADD is deleted. Two things the adjacency folds never needed: a forward scan proving there is no other use, and a watch on the ADD's *source*, which the rewritten accesses read at their own offsets (SP included, where `arm64_gpr_num` returns -1 and the liveness scan is blind). 2,644 of the sites are stack frames, one LLVM shape -- `LocalStackSlotAllocation` inserting a virtual base register off a pre-layout offset estimate that turns out to fit |
| Register-side liveness sweep over SVE/SME | The exhaustive `classify_reg_liveness` cross-check (ARMLINT_LIVENESS_SWEEP) excludes the SVE and SME vector spaces. Under Capstone 6 the property reports **~20M** violations there -- the SME outer-product, gather/scatter and ZA-tile families, plus the FEAT_LSE128/FEAT_THE 128-bit atomics -- every one a genuine disagreement about an instruction armlint does not model. None is reachable today: Capstone 5, the shipped dependency, does not decode them, so the driver skips those words as data and flushes state. The scalar members of the same spaces ARE modelled and swept (SVE INCB/DECB and CLASTA/CLASTB read the GPR they write, LDG replaces only Xt's tag), because 5 does decode those and they appear in hand-written crypto. Not only those: 5 also decodes SVE INDEX and DUP from a GPR (`index z16.s, w20, #1`, `mov z18.s, w6`, in OpenSSL's SVE ChaCha20), MTE's IRG and FP16's `fmov h2, w11`, each with its GPR operand flagless -- a read the sweep's oracle cannot see either.
